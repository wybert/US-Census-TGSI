\begin{abstract}

Understanding subjective well-being (SWB) at fine geographic scales is increasingly important for policy and social science, yet survey-based measures are constrained in temporal frequency, spatial granularity, and cost. We present the US Census-Tract Twitter Sentiment Geographical Index (US-CT-TSGI), a high-resolution sentiment dataset for the United States derived from approximately 2.8 billion geotagged tweets posted between 2010 and 2023. Tweet-level sentiment, scored with a BERT-based model, is assigned to 2020 US Census blocks by point-in-polygon spatial join and aggregated into daily, monthly, and yearly statistics for 8.13 million census blocks --- the finest US census geography, readily aggregated to tracts, counties, or states. To accommodate the spatial uncertainty of tweet locations, we release four confidence-stratified tiers together with a confidence-weighted sentiment mean. We technically validate the dataset along three dimensions: geographic representativeness relative to population (coverage ratio and Gini index), temporal characteristics including the effect of Twitter's 2019 removal of precise geotagging, and external convergence with CDC PLACES community mental-health prevalence. The resulting open dataset complements traditional surveys with timely, high-frequency, neighborhood-scale measures of expressed sentiment for research in public health, urban studies, economics, and political science.

\end{abstract}

\section*{Background \& Summary}


Subjective Well-Being (SWB), encompassing cognitive life evaluations and affective emotional states \cite{dienerSubjectiveWellbeing1984}, remains a critical focus for researchers and policymakers seeking measures beyond traditional economic indicators \cite{jaidkaEstimatingGeographicSubjective2020,deatonIncomeHealthWellbeing2008}. While surveys like the Gallup World Poll \cite{deatonIncomeHealthWellbeing2008} or national panels (e.g., PSID \cite{lucasMeasuringExperientialWellbeing2019}) provide valuable SWB data, they often face limitations in temporal frequency, geographic granularity, and cost \cite{chaiTwitterSentimentGeographical2023a}, particularly for tracking rapid changes or localized phenomena.

The proliferation of social media, combined with advances in Natural Language Processing (NLP), offers a complementary approach \cite{chaiTwitterSentimentGeographical2023a}. Platforms like Twitter capture real-time expressions of public mood, attitude, and feeling. Sentiment analysis techniques allow for the quantification of these expressions \cite{chaiTwitterSentimentGeographical2023a}, and studies have shown correlations between social media sentiment and survey-based SWB measures \cite{jaidkaEstimatingGeographicSubjective2020}. Previous work, including our own Twitter Sentiment Geographical Index (TSGI) \cite{chaiTwitterSentimentGeographical2023a}, demonstrated the feasibility of creating large-scale, multi-lingual sentiment indices at the country and admin-2 (county/city) levels using billions of geotagged tweets \cite{chaiTwitterSentimentGeographical2023a}. These indices have proven useful for different studies ranging from analyzing the sentiment impacts of major events like the COVID-19 pandemic \cite{wangGlobalEvidenceExpressed2022} and the complex spatial relationships between urban sprawl and SWB \cite{wangUrbanSprawlSubjective2025}, to rural–urban differences among social media users \cite{jiangRuralurbanDifferencesDeterminants2025} and global sentiment variations on decentralized finance linked to GDP per capita \cite{chenGlobalPublicSentiment2025}.

However, many social, economic, and policy dynamics occur at much finer geographic scales within countries, particularly within diverse nations like the United States. County-level aggregation can mask significant intra-county heterogeneity. To address this, we develop a new, publicly available Twitter sentiment index aggregated at the US census tract level. Census tracts are designed to be relatively homogeneous units with respect to population characteristics, economic status, and living conditions, making them a highly relevant scale for local analysis. This US Census Tract Twitter Sentiment Geographical Index (US-CT-TSGI) utilizes the same core sentiment analysis methodology as the global TSGI but applies a more granular geocoding process specific to the US administrative boundaries. This dataset provides researchers with a powerful tool to investigate neighborhood-level variations in expressed sentiment and explore their connections to local events, demographic characteristics, environmental factors, and policy interventions.

Our primary contribution is the creation of the first open-source, daily, monthly and yearly Twitter sentiment database resolved to the US census block level (8.13 million blocks nationwide), readily aggregable to census tracts, covering approximately 2.8 billion geotagged tweets from 2010 to 2023. This dataset offers:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item High Granularity: Sentiment data resolved to the census block level (the finest US census geography), enabling aggregation to tracts or any higher unit.
    \item Temporal Frequency: Daily, monthly, and yearly resolution enabling the analysis of short-term fluctuations.
    \item Consistent Methodology: Built upon a validated BERT-based sentiment model \cite{chaiTwitterSentimentGeographical2023a}, ensuring methodological continuity with broader-scale indices.
\end{enumerate}

We anticipate this dataset will be a valuable resource for urban studies, public health, sociology, economics, and political science, enabling novel research into the micro-geographic patterns of expressed sentiment and well-being in the United States.

\subsection*{Data Collection, Cleaning and Sentiment Enhancing}


We apply the same Twitter data archive, preprocessing steps and sentiment analysis methods as detailed in our previous work \cite{chaiTwitterSentimentGeographical2023a} to ensure data quality and consistency with the sentiment model training. In addition, as we focus on the US, we utilized tweets identified as originating within the United States from 2010 to 2023, encompassing approximately 2.8 billion geotagged tweets after initial filtering and cleaning. Volume is negligible in 2010--2011 (fewer than 10$^3$ tweets) and substantive coverage begins in 2012.


\subsection*{Spatial Join and Confidence Scoring}

Tweets were geolocated to U.S. census blocks (the finest granularity of census geography, 2020 TIGER/Line boundaries) via a point-in-polygon spatial join on tweet coordinates (EPSG:4326), assigning each tweet the 15-digit GEOID20 of its containing block.

Geotagged tweets vary in positional precision: some carry device-reported GPS coordinates, while others are resolved from a place or point-of-interest tag to a centroid, with an associated location-uncertainty radius (`spatialerror`, in meters). To quantify how much a tweet's assigned block should be trusted given this uncertainty, we compute a per-tweet spatial-join confidence score $C_i \in \{1.0, 0.8, 0.5, 0.3, 0.15, 0.05\}$ by comparing `spatialerror` against the diameter of the matched block, $d$:
\[
C_i =
\begin{cases}
1.00 & \text{GPS-precise, or spatialerror} < 50\text{m} \\
0.80 & \text{spatialerror} < d/2 \\
0.50 & \text{spatialerror} < d \\
0.30 & \text{spatialerror} < 2d \\
0.15 & \text{spatialerror} < 1000\text{m} \\
0.05 & \text{otherwise}
\end{cases}
\]
Because block size varies enormously (dense urban blocks vs.\ large rural blocks), confidence is scaled relative to the matched block's own diameter rather than a fixed distance: a given uncertainty radius is more likely to place a tweet outside its assigned block when that block is small, and less likely when the block is large. GPS-precise tweets, or those with sub-50m uncertainty, receive the maximum confidence of 1.0 regardless of block size.

\subsection*{Aggregating Sentiment Scores}

Tweet-level sentiment scores (ranging from 0 to 1) were aggregated to the census block level (identified by GEOID20) on daily, monthly, and yearly bases.

To account for the spatial uncertainty of tweet locations, we implemented a multi-tier aggregation strategy based on the confidence score defined above. We generated four sets of statistics for each block-time unit:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Strict}: Includes only tweets with a confidence score of 1.0 (exact match).
    \item \textbf{High}: Includes tweets with confidence $\ge$ 0.8.
    \item \textbf{Medium}: Includes tweets with confidence $\ge$ 0.5.
    \item \textbf{All}: Includes all tweets regardless of confidence.
\end{enumerate}
Each tier is computed as an independent filter-then-aggregate pass over the tweets in a given block-time unit, and the four resulting tables are left-joined on the block-time key (with the `All' tier, which is present for every unit, as the base). Consequently a block-time unit with, e.g., no GPS-exact tweets will have populated `all\_*' fields but null `strict\_*' fields, rather than a zero or an absent row; this should be interpreted as no tweets qualifying for that tier, not as missing data.

For each tier, we calculated the tweet count, unique user count, and descriptive statistics of the sentiment scores (mean, standard deviation, minimum, maximum, and percentiles: 10th, 25th, 50th, 75th, 90th). Additionally, for the 'All' tier, we computed a confidence-weighted mean sentiment score:
\[
\text{Weighted Mean} = \frac{\sum (S_i \times C_i)}{\sum C_i}
\]
where $S_i$ is the sentiment score and $C_i$ is the spatial confidence of tweet $i$.

\section*{Technical Validation}

To ensure the reliability and validity of the US-CT-TSGI dataset, we conducted a comprehensive technical validation across three dimensions: spatial representativeness, temporal stability, and external convergence with established public health indicators. Our validation relies on three external datasets: (1) block-level population counts from the 2020 US Decennial Census \cite{uscensusbureau2020decennial}, (2) TIGER/Line 2020 census tract boundary shapefiles \cite{uscensusbureau2020tigerline} for spatial visualization, and (3) the CDC PLACES dataset (2020--2024 releases) \cite{cdcplaces2024} for tract-level mental health prevalence.

\subsection*{Geographic Coverage and Representativeness}

A critical challenge in social media-based indices is the potential for geographic sampling bias. To assess this, we integrated population counts from the 2020 US Decennial Census at the block level. We calculated the Coverage Ratio (CR) for each census tract (aggregated from blocks) as follows:
\[
CR = \frac{\text{Tweet Count}}{\text{Population}}
\]
Block-level population is available only at the decennial census, and 2010 and 2020 blocks do not share boundaries, so no annually-matched population series exists at this resolution. For the year-2020 CR we pair 2020 tweets with 2020 population (temporally matched). For the all-years (2010--2023) CR, tweets summed across the full window are necessarily normalized against the single 2020 population snapshot, implicitly treating each block's population as constant over the period; we return to this limitation below.

Place-tagged (non-GPS) tweets are geocoded by the source platform to a fixed centroid for the named place (e.g.\ the geographic center of a state or city); when that centroid coordinate falls inside a census block with a very small population, all tweets sharing that place tag are attributed to a single near-empty block, producing an implausible tweet-per-resident ratio that reflects geocoding precision rather than local activity. We detect and mask such blocks by flagging any block with population under 50 and a tweet count exceeding a volume implausible for that population (50,000 over 2010--2023; 5,000 within a single year), and exclude flagged blocks from the national totals used to normalize CR and from all tract-level rollups (choropleth maps, Gini/Lorenz, and the health-indicator correlation below), in addition to the existing $<$20-tweet low-coverage mask.

We visualize the spatial distribution of $\log_2(CR)$ to identify areas of over-representation (positive values) and under-representation (negative values). Across the full 2010--2023 period, tweets were observed in 6.47 million of the 8.13 million census blocks, covering 83,784 of the 84,414 census tracts with land area (99.3\%) after masking (Figure~\ref{fig:log2cr_map}). The spatial pattern shows the expected over-representation of dense urban cores and tourist destinations and under-representation of sparsely populated rural areas.

To quantify the inequality of the tweet distribution relative to population, we computed the Gini coefficient and the Lorenz curve across census tracts (Figure~\ref{fig:lorenz}), for both the full 2010--2023 period and the temporally-matched year-2020 subset (Figure~\ref{fig:lorenz2020}). Over the full period, 83,784 of 83,848 tracts (99.9\%) are reportable, covering 99.99\% of the US population, with Gini $=0.55$ and $\log_2(CR)$ deciles P10/P50/P90 of $-2.36/-0.79/0.68$. For 2020 alone, 52,548 tracts (62.7\%) are reportable, covering 66.3\% of the population, with Gini $=0.93$ and $\log_2(CR)$ deciles $-6.15/-3.79/0.48$ --- both a smaller reportable share and a wider, more negatively-skewed CR distribution than the cumulative all-years figures, consistent with a single year sampling a narrower and less complete slice of geotagged activity. Tweet volume is substantially more concentrated than population in both cases --- a well-known property of social-media data that users should account for, for example by population-weighting or by masking low-coverage units (we flag tracts with fewer than 20 tweets via the \texttt{mask\_low\_coverage} field).

\subsection*{Temporal Stability and Data Consistency}

We examined the national time series of tweet volume and tweet-weighted mean sentiment from 2010 to 2023, and compared the four confidence tiers (Figure~\ref{fig:temporal}). Annual tweet volume varies considerably (from $\sim$32 million in 2016 to $\sim$735 million in 2014), reflecting changes in the underlying collection coverage of the geotagged-tweet archive over time rather than true changes in posting activity; the tweet-weighted mean sentiment is more stable, ranging from 0.54 to 0.71 across years.

The comparison of confidence tiers reveals a structural change in the data that users should be aware of. From 2012 through 2017, essentially all geotagged tweets carried GPS-precise coordinates (the `Strict' tier accounts for 100\% of volume), so the four tiers are identical. Following Twitter's removal of precise (GPS) geotagging in June 2019 \cite{huUnderstandingRemovalPrecise2020}, the GPS-precise share collapses to roughly 30\% in 2018 and 7--11\% from 2019 onward, with the remainder being place-tagged tweets resolved to administrative or point-of-interest centroids. After this transition the `Strict' subset is not only small but also non-representative: its tweet-weighted mean sentiment runs $\sim$0.10 higher than the `All' tier. This motivates our multi-tier design and the confidence-weighted mean: we recommend the confidence-weighted `All' tier for time series spanning the 2019 transition, and caution against treating the post-2019 `Strict' subset as a continuation of the pre-2019 GPS-based series.

\subsection*{Correlation with Public Health Indicators (External Validity)}

As a measure of external validity, we examined the relationship between our Twitter-derived sentiment indices and community-level mental health outcomes. We utilized the CDC PLACES dataset (Local Data for Better Health), specifically the crude prevalence of "Mental health not good for $\ge$14 days" (MHLTH) at the census tract level.

We aggregated our block-level sentiment to the tract level for the years 2020, 2021, and 2022 to match the available CDC data, and computed Pearson and Spearman correlations (both unweighted and population-weighted) between annual mean sentiment and MHLTH prevalence (Table~\ref{tab:correlation}, Figure~\ref{fig:correlation}). For all three years the correlation is negative and highly statistically significant: Pearson $r = -0.047$ in 2020 ($N = 52{,}500$, $p = 8\times10^{-27}$, 95\% CI $[-0.055, -0.038]$), $-0.039$ in 2021 ($N = 48{,}046$, $p = 7\times10^{-18}$), and $-0.042$ in 2022 ($N = 50{,}217$, $p = 6\times10^{-21}$); Spearman coefficients are of the same sign and significance, and population weighting leaves the estimates essentially unchanged. Consistent with prior ecological associations between aggregate social-media sentiment and well-being, the effect sizes are modest, as expected when comparing noisy, self-selected social-media expression against survey-based prevalence at fine spatial resolution.

As a discriminant (placebo) check, we repeated the analysis against an indicator with no \textit{a priori} link to expressed sentiment --- the crude prevalence of recent mammography use (MAMMOUSE). This control yields \textit{positive} correlations of $+0.04$ to $+0.07$ across the three years, opposite in sign to the mental-distress association. The combination of a significant negative correlation with mental distress and a non-negative correlation with the placebo indicator supports the convergent and divergent validity of the index as a proxy for community-level subjective well-being, while underscoring that it should be used as an aggregate signal rather than a precise predictor.

This dataset comprises approximately 2.8 billion geo-tagged tweets in the U.S. from 2010 to 2023, enriched with census-block geography and BERT-based sentiment, and aggregated to daily, monthly, and yearly statistics at the census-block level. To our knowledge it is the first geography-enriched US tweet collection released at this scale and granularity. The block-level aggregates can be rolled up to census tracts (first 11 digits of \texttt{GEOID20}), counties, or states by re-aggregating tweet counts and recomputing tweet- or confidence-weighted sentiment; the data are stored as Parquet and are efficiently queried with tools such as DuckDB, pandas, or polars without loading the full corpus into memory.

We recommend that users observe the following caveats:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Geographic non-representativeness.} Tweet volume is more spatially concentrated than population (tract-level Gini $\approx 0.55$ over 2010--2023; $\approx 0.93$ for 2020 alone). Sparsely populated and rural areas are under-sampled. We recommend population weighting and masking low-coverage units (the \texttt{mask\_low\_coverage} field flags tracts with fewer than 20 tweets or with a coordinate-collapse artifact from place-tag geocoding; see Methods); coverage-ratio fields are provided to support reweighting.
    \item \textbf{Static population baseline for the all-years CR.} Block-level population is only available at the decennial census, so our all-years (2010--2023) Coverage Ratio and Gini normalize 14 years of cumulative tweets against a single 2020 population snapshot, implicitly assuming constant population over the period; blocks with substantial population growth or decline between 2010 and 2023 will have a biased CR. The year-2020 CR/Gini (Gini $\approx 0.93$, above; \texttt{tweet\_count\_2020\_with\_pop\_CR.parquet}) pairs same-year tweets and population and is not subject to this bias; we recommend it, or a user-supplied time-varying population series, for analyses sensitive to this assumption.
    \item \textbf{2019 geotagging regime change.} Following Twitter's June 2019 removal of precise geotagging \cite{huUnderstandingRemovalPrecise2020}, the data shift from predominantly GPS-precise to predominantly place-tagged tweets. The confidence tiers and \texttt{confidence} score capture this; for analyses spanning the transition, use the confidence-weighted `All' tier rather than the `Strict' subset.
    \item \textbf{Variable temporal coverage.} Annual tweet volume reflects the collection coverage of the source archive and varies markedly between years (Figure~\ref{fig:temporal}); 2010--2011 are too sparse for reliable estimates and should be excluded.
    \item \textbf{Demographic skew.} Twitter users are not demographically representative of the general population; expressed sentiment reflects the posting population, not all residents, and should be interpreted as an aggregate, relative signal rather than an absolute measure of well-being.
    \item \textbf{Sentiment model scope.} Scores derive from the BERT-based multilingual model of \cite{chaiTwitterSentimentGeographical2023a}; sarcasm, code-switching, and domain-specific language remain challenging, as for all automated sentiment methods.
\end{enumerate}
